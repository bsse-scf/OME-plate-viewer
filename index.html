In-Browser Plate Viewer

Drop a plate folder to browse and view the plate in the browser.

Data is not uploaded to remote servers and stays on the local machine. Nothing is written, converted or copied.

About this viewer

Using the viewer

dragPan scrollMove through z ctrl + scrollZoom EscLeave the viewer and come back to the plate map

The viewer opens looking down at the plate, one panel. The buttons in the panel's top corner switch to orthogonal or 3-D layouts when you want them.

Click a well on the plate map to open just that well, which loads immediately. Opening the whole plate loads every well at full resolution, so it streams in well by well and a plate of tiled wells is more than the viewer can hold.

What it does

A screening run leaves one XML file describing the plate and a folder of single-plane TIFFs, tens of thousands of them, hundreds of gigabytes. Nothing reads that directly.

This page reads the XML, works out where every field of view sits, and presents the measurement as a virtual OME-Zarr plate: metadata generated on demand, chunks answered by slicing the TIFFs where they already are. A Service Worker serves it over ordinary same-origin HTTP, and Neuroglancer opens it like any other OME-Zarr. 

20251027T134435_10X/     the folder you drop
├─ 00013515.ome.xml      what gets read
└─ Image/                stays exactly where it is
   └─ W0001F0001T0001Z001C1.tif …

What comes out is an OME-Zarr plate laid out as the specification says: a row group, a column group, and the well's image inside it. 

A/                       row A
└─ 1/                    column 1, i.e. well A1
   └─ 0/                 the well's image
      └─ 0/              the pixels, at full resolution

Nothing is uploaded, nothing is converted and nothing is written. Close the tab and every trace of it is gone.

How the plate is put together

  • Fields of view into wells. The stage positions of a well's fields are clustered back onto their acquisition grid, and each is cropped to the grid step so neighbours abut. One field of view is one chunk; nothing is blended and no pixel is resampled.
  • Wells onto the plate. Each well's image carries a translation putting it in its place on the plate, spaced by the widest well plus half again rather than by the millimetres of plastic a real plate would put between them. Opening several wells at once assembles them into one plate.
  • Full resolution only. There is no pyramid. The chunks are the fields of view as acquired, so nothing is ever resampled and what you see is what the microscope recorded. Zoomed out, the viewer reduces on the GPU from what it has loaded, which means a whole plate of tiled wells is more than it can hold at once.
  • Channels. Colours, names and display ranges are written as omero metadata, so Neuroglancer opens one additive layer per channel with the right colour already set.

Which plates work

The viewer reads a plate folder holding OME-XML metadata beside single-plane TIFFs, which is what a high-content screening run typically leaves behind. It works out the plate, its wells and every field of view from that metadata alone.

So far it has been developed and tested against Yokogawa CQ3000 data. Other instruments that write OME metadata in the same shape should follow, but have not been tried, and the reader will say so rather than guess if it meets something it does not recognise.

Which browsers work

Chrome, Edge, Opera, Brave and other Chromium-based browsers.

Nothing is uploaded, so the page has to read the dropped folder where it already sits, which needs the File System Access API, and then serve it to Neuroglancer through a Service Worker. Firefox and Safari give a page only a private storage area of its own, never a folder on your disk, so there is nothing for this viewer to open there.
